“Can we program the weights and skip training?” was a better question than it sounded. We could not skip everything. We could stop asking gradient descent to invent the obvious.

A network with a sensible first move

The user proposed weight-space programming: putting useful behaviour directly into the network rather than waiting for optimization to discover it. Exact mine inference was not a practical hand-wiring target here. The constraint solver combines local equations with global combinatorial counts; a small fixed collection of convolutions does not simply replace that algorithm because we wish very hard.

But action selection offered an easier opportunity. If the mine head already estimates where mines are, why initialize an unrelated policy head to meaningless preferences? The open policy was written as β × mine_logit + residual. A logit is the unnormalized score before conversion into a probability. The residual is a learned correction, with its last layer initialized to zero.

At initialization, that correction contributes nothing. With the programmed mine preference, the policy already means “prefer the likeliest mine.” Training can later learn when local greed is insufficient. This is not an exact strategy encoded in weights; it is a useful default expressed in the architecture. The distinction saves us from calling a starting assumption a solved game.

Learning arithmetic from a teacher

The mine head did still need to learn. Fortunately, we had a teacher that could label public positions: the exact solver. Supervised learning fits network outputs to those labels, instead of waiting for a distant win or loss to explain which uncertain cells looked promising. Distillation turns a slower explicit procedure into a fast approximate predictor.

The first bootstrap used 1,800 solver games, roughly 139,000 positions, and 8,000 training steps. It took about two and a half minutes. The resulting network, without search, won 58% against the greedy solver. After two tiny self-play iterations of 32 games each, the network reached 74%, and MCTS with 100 simulations reached 77.5% in a 200-game evaluation. These were early, pre-reset results, not scores for the final bomb-trained agents.

How can a student beat the solver teaching it? Because the teacher supplies a particular kind of knowledge, not an optimal policy proof. Exact mine probabilities do not settle turn management and information disclosure. A learned action preference can exploit patterns in outcomes even when its mine estimate is approximate. Conversely, beating a greedy solver does not demonstrate exact inference inside the network.

Bootstrap is not sparring

There is an easy verbal trap here. Bootstrap training means supervised imitation of positions and choices from solver-generated games. It does not mean the network is repeatedly playing matches against the solver and learning directly from those encounters. Those matches are evaluations. Self-play is a separate stage, with the network playing both seats and search producing improved targets.

The shared residual body makes useful board features available to several tasks. The mine head predicts spatial uncertainty. The policy scores actions. The value head summarizes the expected result. Their losses provide different teaching signals, and their relative influence can matter. Later experiments with the value-loss weight would test that influence rather than assuming more emphasis must be better.

A head start, not a free lunch

Two network sizes entered the story: 64 channels with four residual blocks, and 32 channels with three. The original architecture diagrams recorded 318,980 and 67,332 parameters respectively. Those historical counts belong to that architecture discussion; adding bomb inputs and a head changes what is being counted. “Small” and “medium” describe architecture choices, not guaranteed standings.

The general trick is modest and reusable: encode a justified starting behaviour, then leave a learnable route away from it. Zero-initializing the last residual layer does exactly that. It removes random nonsense from the initial decision without freezing the policy forever. The programmer supplies the opening sentence; training is still responsible for discovering whether the rest of the paragraph makes sense.
