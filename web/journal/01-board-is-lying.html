The first bug was in the brief: “51×51” turned out to mean 51 mines. Before teaching a machine to play, we had to establish what game we were playing.

A small clarification with large consequences

On November 6, the user asked for an AlphaZero-style AI for one-on-one Minesweeper: a deep neural network, optional search, and a web interface. The ambition was to find as many mines as possible on a “51×51 board.” The director asked a useful question rather than immediately allocating a very large array. The intended reference was classic MSN Minesweeper Flags: a 16×16 board containing 51 mines. First to 26 wins.

This project was built by the user directing AI coding agents. The director delegated work to workers; the user supplied goals, challenged assumptions, and changed the rules. That distinction matters. The story is not that an autonomous system woke up and invented a game. It is that someone kept asking pointed questions while a collection of coding assistants tried to turn the answers into working software.

Forget everything you know about avoiding mines

Ordinary Minesweeper rewards survival. Here, mines are the prize. Claim one and you score a point and keep the turn. Open a safe cell and its clue becomes public, with zero regions flood-filling; then the turn passes. A safe move may hand your opponent exactly the information they need. Congratulations on your excellent reconnaissance. Unfortunately, you conducted it for the other team.

The final game also gives each player one bomb. It clears a clipped 5×5 area, claims its unopened mines, reveals safe cells, and ends the turn. We will return to that addition later. For now, notice the strategic ingredients already present without it: immediate reward, extra turns, information revealed to both players, and a race to a majority rather than a requirement to solve the whole board.

A cell’s mine probability is therefore useful but not the entire strategy. Clicking the most likely mine maximizes a local chance of scoring. Winning asks a larger question: what sequence of information and turn ownership follows? Those questions can disagree. The greedy exact solver became our sensible baseline, not a theorem that the game was solved.

The AlphaZero-shaped hole

The familiar AlphaZero recipe combines a policy network, a value network, tree search, and self-play. Policy suggests moves; value estimates outcomes; search spends computation testing the suggestions. Self-play turns those searches into training examples. It is an appealing loop because the player can improve without a human writing a catalogue of good tactics.

There is a catch. The board is hiding its mines. In a perfect-information game, a search can simulate the actual state available to both players. Giving this search the real mine layout would give the AI an unfair private answer sheet. Searching one invented board as though it were certain would make a different mistake: confusing a possibility with knowledge.

The design therefore separated the public position from a possible hidden layout. An exact constraint solver reasons from visible clues and the remaining mine count. It can produce probabilities and sample layouts consistent with what everyone has seen. Each search simulation receives a sampled layout; the tree is organized around public states rather than an omniscient map.

Three jobs, not one magic network

The early network had three heads sharing a residual-network body: mine probabilities, policy, and value. These are related jobs, but their labels mean different things. Mine probabilities describe uncertainty about the board. Policy describes a choice. Value estimates how the game will finish from the current player’s perspective. A later bomb-policy head extended the action choice without making hidden mines public.

This division gave us an important shortcut. Exact inference already supplied teaching labels for the mine head; there was no reason to rediscover arithmetic entirely through wins and losses. The policy could start with a programmed preference for likely mines, then learn a residual correction. Search could sample hidden worlds without storing one as the truth.

None of this guarantees strong play. It does give each component an intelligible responsibility, which is helpful when the results later become stubborn. The project began with a smaller board than the first sentence suggested and a larger epistemology problem than the phrase “AlphaZero-like” admitted. Asking what the agent is allowed to know was the first real move.
