The training counter kept going up. The win rate mostly stopped. This is where a success story either acquires error bars or starts inventing excuses.

A flat chart can hide a moving player

Greedy evaluation against the solver ran every five iterations over 100 games. Medium rose from roughly 68% to around 80% by iterations 15–25, then wandered through a noisy 75–88% range. That looked like a plateau. But a single opponent is a narrow measuring instrument, so promoted milestone generations played a round-robin, with 20 greedy games per pair.

Medium’s fitted Elo climbed from 1058 at g0 to 1180 at g10, 1216 at g20, 1251 at g30, 1281 at g50, and 1298 at g70, before 1294 at g80. Small went from 1019 at g0 to 1228 at g20, 1314 at g30, and 1311 at g50. These milestone results showed genuine earlier progress that the solver-only view understated. They also made the later flattening harder to dismiss.

The clues did not name one culprit

Medium’s value mean-squared error improved from about 0.59 to 0.29. Something was still learning. Policy targets were sharp, representing roughly five effective actions out of around 190–330 legal choices. Search looked prior-dominated. Longer games explained some slowdown. Medium tended to bomb early; small saved its bomb. Each observation suggested a hypothesis, but none was itself a diagnosis.

MCTS-100 was not clearly helping: medium g70 search rated 1261 against greedy’s 1298. On g80, search against its own greedy policy scored 54% with 100 simulations and 55% with 200, at half the speed. Setting first-play urgency to zero reached 56%, but a 160-game extension produced 88 versus 89 in the comparison, not credible confirmation of a gain.

Change one thing, keep the snapshot

Controlled trials started from identical snapshots and ran 15 iterations per branch. Medium’s value-loss weight 1.0 versus 0.25 produced no head-to-head gain: 46%. Held-out value MSE was worse, 1.14 versus 0.69. We kept 0.25. Giving a task more loss weight had not made its predictions better on the relevant held-out evidence.

Small’s lower learning rate, 2e-4 instead of 1e-3, looked more promising: MCTS scored 57.5% over 400 games. But the confirmation confidence interval touched 50%, and greedy showed no gain. We kept 1e-3. “Promising, unproven” is a useful experimental outcome, even if it makes a disappointing victory banner.

When terrible results are good debugging clues

Gumbel root search initially won only 18–22% against its own greedy policy. That was implausibly poor, so the result triggered debugging rather than an immediate declaration that the algorithm was bad. Exploration noise had remained enabled during evaluation, and Q scaling was wrong. After fixes, it scored 50.5%: cheap, but not stronger.

The final practical choice was PUCT-100 with tree reuse and four-leaf batching. It reduced cost without demonstrating a strength gain over old search. That is a different success criterion, and reporting it separately stops a throughput improvement from quietly becoming a playing-strength claim.

The ending we actually measured

Training stopped at medium iteration 161 and small 115. The final round-robin covered 12 agents, 20 games per pair, 1,320 games. Medium g161 greedy and MCTS both rated 1301±64. Small g115 greedy rated 1321±64 and MCTS 1308±64. Medium g113 greedy was 1308; small g82 MCTS was 1314. Solver remained 1000, random −211.

In 200 paired head-to-head games, medium versus small MCTS won 48.0%, with a 41.5–55.0% interval. Greedy won 47.5%, interval 40.5–54.5%. The defensible conclusion was that the sizes were roughly equal, progress had plateaued around g50–80, and search still added nothing measurable. Size was not the demonstrated bottleneck.

Possible next experiments include solver probabilities as an input and a richer value target such as score margin. They remain ideas, not results. The plateau did not supply a tidy villain. It supplied something better than a made-up breakthrough: a smaller set of claims that survived contact with the matches.
