With a hand-written expert winning 85% against the solver, the obvious next move was the AlphaZero playbook: teach a network to imitate the expert, then let search push it further. The network learned quickly. Search, in two different forms, made it worse. This chapter explains why: a measurement whose noise is larger than the differences it has to detect.

A network that sees probabilities

The old networks had to infer mine probabilities from raw clues, a job the exact solver does perfectly. The new network gets them for free. Its input is the 20 public planes plus three solver planes: mine probability, certain mine and certain safe. All of them are functions of the public board, so nothing hidden leaks in. The body is a 96-channel, 8-block ResNet. It has an open head and a bomb head that share one softmax over 512 actions, plus a value head.

We generated 6,000 expert-versus-expert games (646,074 positions). To keep the data varied, each seat drew its own α and bomb reserve, and the first 20 moves were sampled from the expert's scores at temperature 0.3. The labels are that softmax. A two-minute pilot trained on the first 1,000 games already won 84% against the solver with its greedy policy, the same as the expert, at a fraction of the cost per move.

The value head memorizes games

Each game contributes about a hundred positions, and all of them share one outcome label, z∈{−1,+1}. A big enough network can learn to recognize the game instead of judging the position. We held out every 50th game, never single positions, so the leak shows up clearly:

By the end, the held-out value error (0.82) was worse than always predicting a draw. The policy had no such problem. The finished network, net-0, won 78.2% against the solver over 600 games and 39.3% against the expert over 300. It imitates the average of randomly configured experts with exploration noise, not the best configuration.

Search on a bad compass

Public-state MCTS with 128 simulations leans on the value head at every leaf. With the pilot network it won 73.5% against the solver, worse than the same network's greedy 84%. That repeats the old project's lesson: search amplifies its evaluator, errors included.

So we removed the value head from the loop. The rollout agent takes the policy's top six moves and samples 32 layouts from the exact posterior. It plays every move on every layout, then finishes each game with the greedy policy for both sides. All six candidates share the same layouts. The rollout policy sees only public states; a sampled layout only decides what chance reveals. It is honest, unbiased, and expensive: about 20,000 positions per decision.

Against the expert it won 34.2% (120 games, 95% CI 25–43%). That is no better than the bare policy.

The noise floor

A full playout ends in a win or a loss. With 32 rollouts, the mean outcome of one candidate has standard error

SE=1−μ2n≈0.932≈0.17

on the ±1 scale, or about eight points of win probability. Neighbouring candidates in Minesweeper Flags usually differ by a point or two. Shared layouts cancel some of the noise, but most of a game's luck happens long after the decision being judged. The agent was ranking moves with a ruler whose markings were wider than the objects.

The expert's formula looks only one turn ahead, but every term in it is small and exact: a probability, a count of certain mines, the value of one guess. The two-ply expert keeps that style and adds the opponent's best reply. Head to head it beat the one-ply expert 63% (200 games, 95% CI 56.5–69.5%). In this game, a short, precise horizon beat a long, noisy one.

What we changed

The next teacher is the two-ply expert itself, with fixed best settings. It labels each position with its chosen move, and 3,000 games are generating as this is written, about ten hours of CPU. We also batched the solver for rollouts, which was only 1.47× faster: hard early-game frontiers, not Python overhead, dominated the cost. Measured against the 3000 target, the honest status is this: the strongest agent so far still loses about one game in seven to the solver.
