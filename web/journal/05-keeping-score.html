A leaderboard needs a ruler. Choosing the player who loses almost everything as the ruler turned out to be a poor way to measure the room.

Why not make random zero?

The arena needed to compare agents with very different methods: random clicks, the greedy exact solver, a network acting directly, and a network using search. Raw win rates are useful for a particular matchup, but a growing collection of pairings calls for a shared scale. Elo provides a convenient language: differences in rating describe expected performance, not an absolute amount of intelligence.

The obvious anchor was random. The problem was that random lost essentially all its games against stronger opponents. In a logistic rating model, a perfect loss record does not pin down a finite distance. Moving the loser farther down keeps making those results more probable. A baseline that almost never wins is good for checking that something works and bad for precisely measuring how much better it is.

We anchored the greedy solver at 1000 instead. It was a useful, competitive reference with a clear definition: reason exactly about mine probabilities, then make a greedy choice. Its fixed rating defines the origin of the scale. It does not declare that its strength has no conceptual uncertainty or that all future players must stay close to it.

One fit for the whole arena

The arena uses Bradley–Terry maximum likelihood on an Elo scale. It fits ratings jointly from the recorded match outcomes: a higher-rated player should be more likely to beat a lower-rated one, and the fit seeks differences that explain the collected evidence. It is not simply adding a fixed number of points after each click or each newly completed match.

A weak Gaussian prior, with standard deviation 800, keeps extreme estimates finite when evidence is sparse. A 20–0 record should suggest a substantial difference, but it cannot tell us that the gap is literally infinite. Regularization admits that distinction. Confidence intervals then remind us that a neat integer on the leaderboard may have a very untidy range of plausible values.

The first leaderboard put MCTS at 1377, exploratory MCTS with c_puct 2.5 at 1272, the network at 1168, the bootstrap network at 1089, the small 32×3 network at 1056, the solver at 1000, and random at −51. These belong to the early lineup. They are not a single timeless ranking that can be pasted beside every later checkpoint.

Names should identify a player, not a moving target

A trained network changes every iteration. If an arena row simply says “medium,” which weights did it use? If “latest” silently changes tomorrow, yesterday’s match still belongs to yesterday’s player. The naming scheme therefore encodes architecture, training provenance, and search configuration, for example r64x4-bs8k-sp2.mcts100-c1.5.

Read that as a 64-channel, four-block residual network; an 8,000-step bootstrap; two self-play iterations; then MCTS with 100 simulations and exploration constant 1.5. Checkpoints are immutable identities, not aliases overwritten whenever training saves. The ledger was migrated to preserve that relationship. A rating without a reproducible player attached is mostly decorative typography.

Categories are shelves; generations are books

The interface later organized six categories: random, solver, and the two network sizes with either greedy or MCTS action selection. A category is a convenient way to browse a family. A generation identifies completed training: generation zero is bootstrap, and generation k is self-play iteration k. The greedy and search versions of an architecture can share the same underlying weights.

That separation lets Play select a category while preserving the exact generation used for a particular game. Arena results belong to concrete generations, not a category whose default can advance. It also makes comparisons more honest: changing search settings is not the same intervention as changing the network, even if both affect the displayed player name.

Reading the ruler carefully

Later milestone round-robins revealed progress that the solver win-rate chart partly hid. But close ratings still need cautious interpretation. The final medium and small agents were separated by much less than their roughly ±64 confidence half-widths. A slightly higher central estimate does not award a scientifically defensible crown.

Elo is a summary of matches, not a substitute for their design. Opponents, pair counts, uncertainty, and fixed identities all matter. The arena’s real achievement was not producing impressive four-digit numbers. It was making it harder to accidentally compare yesterday’s student with today’s search settings and call the difference learning.
