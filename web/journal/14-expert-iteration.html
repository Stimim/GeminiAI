The pieces finally fitted together. A short search that beats its own policy, and a network that copies a teacher almost perfectly, make a loop: search, copy, repeat. The first turn of that loop worked. Then the machine started quietly killing our workers.

Search, copy, repeat

Expert iteration alternates two operators. Search turns a policy into a slightly stronger player; distillation turns that player back into a policy that costs a millisecond per move:

The starting point was net-1, the network distilled from the two-ply expert. On the ledger it won 182 of 200 against the solver and 126 of 200 against the one-ply expert, rating 1404 ± 49. That made it the strongest agent so far, at a thousandth of its teacher's cost.

The rollout search on top of net-1 labelled 900 games. net-2 was fine-tuned on those labels plus the two-ply expert's games, then played net-1 greedily on 400 shared layouts. It won 56.5% (800 games, 95% CI 52.9–60.0%). The search's advantage over its own policy, 55.7% in the previous chapter, carried over almost entirely into a policy that needs no search.

The silent killer

While the first iteration ran, a benchmark sat for four and a half hours without printing a line. Training jobs died after five seconds with exit code 143, a polite SIGTERM. The culprit was earlyoom. It is configured to terminate Python processes when free memory falls below 10%. Every worker that loads PyTorch and CUDA holds about 1.4 GB, and we had eight teacher workers and six benchmark workers on a 16 GB machine.

The kills explained the hang. When multiprocessing.Pool loses a worker, the task that worker held is never retried and never reported. The pool waits for it forever. Both the benchmark and the data generator now use ProcessPoolExecutor, which raises BrokenProcessPool instead. Concurrent CUDA processes are capped at five. A crash you can see is a bug; a silent hang is a lost afternoon.

A compressed ruler

The second ledger round put net-2 at the top: 90 of 100 against the solver, 64 of 100 against the one-ply expert, 56 of 100 against net-1. Its rating is 1394 ± 52; net-1 slipped to 1360 after a weaker 78 of 100 against the solver in the same round. Hundred-game samples move ratings by tens of points.

The bigger lesson is the shape of the scale. Head to head, net-2 beats the one-ply expert by about 100 Elo. Against the solver, the two score 90% and 86%, a difference of only about 70:

In chapter 11's loss analysis each player made about 18 genuine guesses per game. Some games are lost to those dice however well the rest is played. As skill rises, the win rate against a fixed anchor saturates below 100%, so the ratings near the top are compressed. To reach 3000 an agent would have to lose about one game in a hundred thousand to an opponent that is often lucky. Each loop iteration buys tens of Elo; the target needs another 1,600. We are running the loop anyway and will report where it stops.
