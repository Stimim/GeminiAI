The user added one bomb per player. Suddenly the network needed another head, the action space doubled, and “take the mine and keep going” acquired an explosive exception.

A small button with a large rules footprint

A bomb clears a 5×5 square centred on the chosen cell, clipped at board edges. It claims all unopened mines in that area for the bomber and reveals safe cells, including ordinary zero flood-fills. Each player gets one per match. Unlike a normal mine claim, detonation consumes the turn. You can take several points at once, but you hand over the next decision.

That last sentence prevents the bomb from being merely “click many times.” A normal mine can preserve a productive chain of moves. A bomb can end that chain, reveal information, or spend a resource that would be more valuable later. The policy must compare an immediate spatial bundle with an ordinary opening and with saving its one-use option.

The solver baseline uses a concrete heuristic: bomb when the expected mine haul reaches at least three, subject to its ordinary action-selection rules. It is a baseline trade-off, not a proof of optimal timing. The expectation comes from summing mine probabilities over the clipped window. Linearity of expectation makes that sum useful without assuming the cells’ mine events are independent.

Represent the choice the player actually has

The network received two extra input planes: bomb available for me, and bomb available for the opponent. Those are public resources and strategically relevant. Two boards with the same visible clues can be different positions if one player has already spent their bomb. Omitting inventory would ask the value and policy heads to average across situations they ought to distinguish.

A second per-cell policy head scores bomb centres. The open and bomb scores participate in one masked softmax over 512 action slots: 256 ordinary openings and 256 bomb centres on the default board. Masking removes illegal choices before selecting an action. Separate heads do not mean separately normalized decisions with an arbitrary extra rule deciding whether to bomb.

That joint distribution is an important modelling detail. The policy must compare “open here” with “bomb there” on the same action scale. If each head independently declared its favourite action nearly certain, the model would still lack an answer to the actual question presented by the user interface.

Program the obvious part, again

The bomb head began with another weight-programming trick: expected mines in the 5×5 window, minus three. Like the mine-logit path in the open policy, this gives the untrained network a meaningful preference rather than a spatially random urge to detonate. The subtraction encodes a conservative starting trade-off; learning remains free to change the preference.

It does not magically solve the turn cost. An expected haul ignores many details about which safe clues will be revealed, who gets to exploit them, and whether later positions offer a better window. Those are reasons to learn a residual policy and a value estimate, not reasons to pretend the initial score is exact strategy.

Transfer, then a clean reset

The first bomb-capable network was transferred from the classic network. Reusing features was a practical way to get the new rules working. But transfer history complicates a comparison: did one player benefit from earlier training that the other never saw? The interface explained the transferred tag, and then the networks were reset from scratch for a clearer experiment.

Both sizes used a shared corpus of 2,000 bomb-solver games, roughly 112,000 positions. Each bootstrap took about two minutes. The medium 64×4 network won 68% against the solver; the small 32×3 network won 52.5%. Those fresh starts, not the transferred model, became the reference point for the longer self-play runs.

The classic no-bomb mode was removed to simplify the active project. Its games were archived, not deleted. That is a useful distinction in an experimental system: a clean current product does not require erasing the evidence of what came before. It does require not mixing old-rule results into a new-rule claim.

A rule change reaches everywhere

Bombs changed more than the board animation. Public state needed inventory, action encoding needed a second action family, training needed joint targets, search needed the correct turn transition, and the baseline needed a policy for spending its resource. A conspicuous interface feature can be the visible tip of a fairly deep consistency problem.

Later, the medium network tended to bomb early while the small network saved its bomb. That behavioural difference was interesting, but it did not establish that either style was better. The final comparison found the sizes roughly equal. Sometimes the explosion is the most dramatic thing on the screen and still not the variable deciding the experiment.
