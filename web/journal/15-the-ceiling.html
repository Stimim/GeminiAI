Five turns of the loop later, two curves have flattened. Each new network barely beats the last, and every new champion beats the solver at about the same rate. This chapter measures both plateaus and states plainly what they mean for the 3000-Elo target.

The loop runs out of teacher

Every iteration gates the new network against its teacher in a greedy match on shared layouts. A win rate above 50% promotes it. The first step was large; the rest hover at a coin flip:

The individual gates are too noisy to read alone. A 1,000-game rematch showed that iteration 3 really is better than net-2, at 54.7%. Across three iterations that is roughly +33 Elo, against +45 for the single first step. The reason showed up when we asked the teacher directly. Two-turn rollouts on top of net-2 won only 48.7% against net-2's own greedy policy (300 games); a search twice as wide won 53.0%, not significantly more. Once the student has absorbed what a short look-ahead can see, the look-ahead has nothing left to teach.

What 3000 would need

The win rate against the solver is the quantity the target is anchored to, and it is saturating around 85–90%. Bradley–Terry turns any win rate w against the anchor into a gap:

Δ=400log10w1−w

Ninety percent is +382, 99% is +798, and 99.999% is +2000. Every extra 400 points demands roughly a tenfold drop in losses. Our losses come largely from guessing luck that the winner of a fair game cannot remove. Chapter 11 measured about three mines of bad luck in a typical loss. We found no evidence that any agent playing only from public information can lose fewer than a few games in a hundred to the greedy solver.

How much of a loss is dice?

To check that claim we audited 600 games between net-3 and the solver. For every guess we recorded the mine probability and whether it hit; for every bomb, the expected and actual mines. A player's luck is the sum of actual minus expected, in mines:

Half of the losses would have been wins if both sides had hit exactly their expected number of mines. Those 41 games, 6.8% of the total, were decided by dice that no public-information strategy controls. This is an approximation: a different player would have faced different guesses. But it puts the ceiling of a fair player against this solver somewhere around 93%, about +450 Elo or a rating near 1450. The luck difference also spreads by 3.8 mines from game to game.

So the honest scoreboard is this. The strongest agent, net-3, plays a fair game in about a millisecond per move. It is rated about 1395, roughly +395 over the solver, +60–75 over the best earlier self-play networks and about +90 over the hand-written expert. The expert-iteration loop keeps running in the background, and further gains will be reported. But the distance from 1400 to 3000 is not a matter of more compute. Under this scale, with this game's luck, it is the distance between winning nine games in ten and winning ninety-nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine in a hundred thousand.
