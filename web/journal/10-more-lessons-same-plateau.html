“Does self-play ever choose moves with lower policy value?” asked the user. Yes. The more awkward question was whether choosing more of them would teach it anything useful.

A policy is not a commandment

A policy probability is not an action’s estimated payoff. It is the network’s preference before search. Self-play can disobey it in three ways: Dirichlet noise mixes into the root prior at every move (α 0.15, 25% noise); PUCT explores during search; and the played move is sampled in proportion to visit counts, at temperature 1, for the first 30 moves. That counter includes both players, not 30 moves each. Games typically take about 80–110 moves; afterwards, play chooses the most-visited action. Evaluation uses no noise and chooses argmax rather than sampling.

So the answer was not “we forgot exploration.” It was “perhaps the existing exploration is insufficient.” The distinction matters: turning a knob is an intervention, not a diagnosis.

First, check that the knob is connected

The exploration trial forked medium g161 from SHA256-identical weights, optimizer state, and replay. Control kept α 0.15 and 30 sampling moves; treatment used α 0.03 and 60. The smaller α makes Dirichlet draws spikier, not simply quieter: AlphaZero’s rough rule is about 10 divided by the legal-action count. Both branches ran 20 iterations concurrently, each with four CPU workers and one trainer.

The treatment really changed behaviour. Moves disagreeing with the prior argmax rose from 30.5% to 56.6%. Effective target actions increased from 6.2 to 7.2; mean game length fell from 91 to 71 moves; bombs per game rose from 1.87 to 1.98. Before asking whether a treatment improves strength, check that it actually administers the proposed medicine.

Strength declined in point estimate, without establishing a regression: treatment beat control 44.0% in greedy play and 45.0% with MCTS. Against starting g161, treatment scored 44.0% greedy while control scored 51.0% [45.0–57.0]. Held-out value MSE was worse too: 0.88 versus 0.62 at g180. Control g181 was promoted. More wandering had produced different games, not a demonstrably better traveller.

Give the student more questions

Next came KataGo-style auxiliary targets. A score-margin head predicted the final mover-relative score difference divided by 26, trained with mean-squared error. A per-cell ownership head classified true mines as claimed by the mover, claimed by the opponent, or unclaimed, including bomb claims, using cross-entropy. Safe cells were not ownership examples.

The new final layers started at zero. Legacy replay lacked these labels, so its auxiliary losses were masked, not filled with invented answers. The architecture received its own key, r64x4aso. Search remained unchanged: it still used the win/loss value, not the margin prediction. This tested richer training supervision, not a new search objective.

Arm one used loss weights 0.10 and 0.05. Its auxiliary contribution was about 0.025 against 2.81 from existing losses: roughly 0.9%. The director judged that too weak to test the idea and sent it back. Results were unconvincing anyway: 47.0% greedy and 47.5% MCTS against control g201. Adding two heads had mostly demonstrated that the network could wear a very small hat.

A meaningful dose, then a second opinion

Arm two restarted from the same g181 snapshot, reusing control g201. Weights 2.0 and 1.5 brought auxiliary losses to roughly 18–20% after replay turnover. Ownership accuracy reached about 76%; margin error was around 4–5 score points. Those tasks were being learned. Held-out win/loss value error, however, was mostly worse than control: 0.96 versus 0.54 at g195.

Greedy scored 48.5% against control; MCTS scored 50.5%. That borderline-positive search result triggered an independent 400-game confirmation bank. It returned 49.5% [44.7–54.5]. Control g201 stayed promoted; auxiliary heads remain available in code, off by default.

What the plateau has ruled out

Matches used paired layouts with alternating seats. Bootstrap intervals resampled whole layout pairs, preserving that dependence. A fresh confirmation bank checked the tempting result rather than asking the same games for a more flattering answer. Wide intervals do not prove equality; they do limit victory speeches.

Value-loss weight, exploration, and richer targets have now failed to move the plateau. The control lineage itself scores only about 48.5–51% against its starting snapshots. Our working hypothesis—not a finding—is that the network is limited by what it can infer from the board, rather than by the training signal. Next candidate: provide the solver’s exact mine probabilities as input planes. Opponent diversity is another possibility.

The roadmap also holds playout-cap randomization, small-network learning rate, compiled hot loops, free-threaded workers, an SPRT benchmark, and this journal. Hosting preparation was removed at the user’s request. That is a list of possible work, not promised breakthroughs. For now, three plausible levers have moved, the plateau has not, and the error bars have earned their keep.
