The new brief was blunt: build an AI rated 3000 Elo on the existing scale, where the greedy solver is pinned at 1000. That means scoring about 99.999% against the solver. Before writing code, we did the arithmetic. Then we stopped asking the network to discover the game's central idea on its own and wrote it down.

What 3000 means

The Bradley–Terry model behind our ratings gives player A an expected score of

E=11+10(RB−RA)/400

A 2000-point gap therefore means E=1/(1+10−5): one loss in about a hundred thousand games. The rating fit also has a Gaussian prior with an 800-Elo standard deviation, which keeps perfect records finite. Even an agent that never lost would need tens of thousands of consecutive wins against the solver before the fit placed it at 3000. Minesweeper Flags also has real luck: early guesses run at a 20% mine density. The target may be out of reach. We kept the scale anyway, as the user chose, and decided to report exactly how far real strength gets.

The board is shared, so every clue is a gift

Both players see the same public board. When you open a safe cell, its number goes to the opponent as well, and it is their move. The greedy solver ignores this: it opens the cell most likely to be a mine. If that guess misses, it often reveals exactly the clue that makes three neighbouring mines certain, and hands them over.

The expert agent scores each move as what I expect to gain minus what I expect to give away. For opening cell c with mine probability pc:

V(c)=pc1−pc−𝔼n∣safe[Sn+αGn]

The first term is a geometric series. A hit keeps the turn and, roughly, offers another guess of the same quality: p+p2+…=p/(1−p). In the second term, n is the revealed number (or flood region); Sn counts the mines it would make certain for the opponent, and Gn is the value of their best remaining guess. The distribution of n comes from layouts sampled consistently with the clues. Certain mines are always claimed first; claiming one reveals nothing and keeps the turn. Bombs score their expected mines, minus the same gift, minus a reserve value for keeping the bomb.

Speed came first

Each decision asks the solver about hundreds of hypothetical boards, and the pure-Python solver made that cost about a second per move. We ported it to C, loaded through ctypes and compiled on first import. It matches the Python probabilities to 1e-9, and it samples uniformly from every enumerated solution rather than from a 64-entry reservoir. Real game positions take 56 µs on average, and all of them were solved exactly. An expert decision fell from about one second to about 30 ms.

Tuning taught a different lesson

The first prototype won 80.8% over 120 games. On the fast solver, the default settings won 84.0% over 600. Sweeping α, the probability cap, the bomb reserve, the number of sampled layouts and the candidate count moved the result by only a few points. Doubling α to 2 hurt (78.2%), and a stingier bomb policy helped (85.7% with reserve 3). The informative failure was that more samples (82.2%) and more candidate cells (80.3%) did not help; the point estimates were even lower. When a model is wrong, examining more options mostly finds more ways to fool it. The heuristic misses the opponent's reply, the next reply, and the fact that the score near 26 matters more than the mine margin.

Next: let search check the formula

About 84% means a loss in roughly one game in six, nowhere near one in a hundred thousand. Where do the losses come from? We tracked every mine in 300 expert-versus-solver games (bomb reserve 3). Comparing mines hit against mines expected from each guess's probability separates luck from gifts:

Losses are partly dice: about three mines of guessing luck. But in those games the solver also received twice as many certain mines as usual. That half is skill, so it can still be improved.

Because every hidden fact is shared chance, not private knowledge, a search over public states is sound: sample a layout from the exact posterior, play the tree, and back the result up. The plan is to bootstrap a larger probability-aware network from expert games, then let public-state MCTS improve on the expert.
